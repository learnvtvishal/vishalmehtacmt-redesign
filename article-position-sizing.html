Risk management

Position sizing: the one rule that keeps you in the game.

Your stop-loss, not your conviction, should decide how much you buy.

VM
By Vishal Mehta, CMT· 19 October 2026·5 min read

Ask a room of traders what they spend most of their time on and the answer is almost always the same: finding entries. Which setup, which indicator, which breakout. Ask them how they decide how much to buy, and the room goes quiet. "Whatever feels right." "Depends on how confident I am." "Usually 100 shares."

In more than two decades of trading and teaching, I have seen far more accounts damaged by poor sizing than by poor entries. A decent idea traded too large can still knock you out. An average idea traded at the right size lets you survive long enough to get better. That is why position sizing is the first rule I teach, before any chart pattern.

Start with risk per trade

Before you look at a chart, decide one number: the most you are willing to lose on a single trade if you are wrong. Many professional traders keep this to around 1% of trading capital, and rarely more than 2%.

It sounds small. It is meant to. Every trader runs into losing streaks; five or six losses in a row is ordinary, not rare. At 1% risk, six straight losses leave you down roughly 6% and fully able to carry on. At 10% risk, the same streak removes nearly half the account.

Risk per trade is not the amount you invest. It is the amount you lose if your stop-loss is hit. The two are very different numbers.

The position sizing formula

Once you know your risk budget and where your stop-loss belongs on the chart, quantity is pure arithmetic:

Quantity = (Capital × Risk %) ÷ (Entry − Stop-loss)
Round down to a whole number. Never round up.

The first part of the formula is your rupee risk budget. The second part is how much you lose per share if the stop is hit. Divide one by the other and you get the number of shares that keeps your loss inside the budget.

A worked example

Say your trading capital is ₹5,00,000 and you risk 1% per trade. You plan to buy at ₹500 with a stop-loss at ₹490, a level chosen because the chart says the idea is wrong below it.

Risk budget (₹5,00,000 × 1%)₹5,000
Risk per share (₹500 − ₹490)₹10
Quantity (₹5,000 ÷ ₹10)500 shares
Position value (500 × ₹500)₹2,50,000
Loss if stop-loss is hit₹5,000

Notice what happened. You deploy half your capital, yet you only risk 1% of it. If the stop were twice as far away, at ₹480, the same formula would give you 250 shares. A wider stop means a smaller position; a tighter stop allows a larger one. The risk stays constant. That is the whole point.

Why it matters: the maths of recovery

Losses and gains are not symmetric. If your account falls 10%, you need about 11% to get back. Lose 20% and you need 25%. Lose 50% and you need 100%, a doubling, just to return to where you started.

Position sizing is what stops a normal losing streak from turning into one of those deep holes. It does not make you right more often. It makes being wrong affordable, which is what gives your edge, if you have one, enough trades to show up. You can see the full table in the drawdown recovery calculator.

Common position sizing mistakes

  • Sizing by conviction. Taking a bigger position because you "really like" a trade. The market does not know how you feel.
  • Moving the stop to fit the size. Deciding the quantity first, then squeezing the stop-loss closer so the risk looks acceptable. The stop belongs where the idea is invalidated, not where it is convenient.
  • Fixed quantity on every trade. Always buying 100 shares means a ₹50 stock and a ₹2,000 stock carry wildly different risk.
  • Ignoring gaps and costs. A stop-loss is a plan, not a guarantee. Prices can gap past it, and brokerage and taxes add up, so leave some margin in your budget.
  • Increasing size after losses. Trying to "win it back" by doubling up is the fastest route to the deep end of the recovery table.

Make it a habit

The traders who last are not the ones with the cleverest entries. They are the ones who do this small calculation before every single trade, especially the ones they feel sure about. Write your risk percentage at the top of your trading plan, calculate quantity from your stop every time, and log it in your journal next to the result.

Key takeaways

  1. Decide your risk per trade first; around 1% of capital is a common professional benchmark.
  2. Quantity = (capital × risk %) ÷ (entry − stop-loss), rounded down.
  3. Place the stop where your idea is wrong, then let it set the size, never the other way round.
  4. Small, consistent risk keeps drawdowns shallow, and shallow drawdowns are far easier to recover from.
